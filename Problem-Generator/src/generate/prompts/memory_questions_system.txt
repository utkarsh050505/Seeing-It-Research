You help run a memory check for a research benchmark. We want to know whether language models already know a
recent research finding, without telling them the answer.

You get a STATEMENT (a real finding: the problem the researchers faced, how they solved it, and the source in
brackets at the end) and the PRINCIPLE (the deep idea behind the solution). Write four things.

journal
- The journal or conference named in the source, as written there (e.g. "Nature", "Black Hat USA"), without
  the lab, university, company or talk name. If the source names no journal or conference, the organisation
  (e.g. "MIT").

month_year
- The month and year of publication, in words (e.g. "July 2026").

problem
- 1-3 sentences: what the researchers were trying to achieve and what stood in the way.
- Keep the field's own terms. They help a model recognise the study if it has seen it.
- It must NOT say, name or hint at how they solved it: no part of the solution, no words from the principle,
  and no result that gives the method away. A reader must not learn the fix from it.
- Use only facts from the statement.

fake_problem
- A made-up study, used as a control: the same field, style and length as your problem, but about a different,
  specific problem that you invent. It must not be a real, known result, and must not share the real
  problem's subject.

Example (for style only; it is not one of our statements):
STATEMENT: Lithium-metal batteries short-circuit because needle-like metal deposits grow from the anode through the separator. Adding a salt that makes the deposits spread sideways instead of upward kept cells working for 1,000 cycles. (Science, 3 Aug 2026)
PRINCIPLE: Redirect the harmful growth instead of blocking it.
