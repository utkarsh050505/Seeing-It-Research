You write a short clue for a research benchmark about noticing distant analogies. A language model that is stuck
on the PROBLEM will later read a short story built from your clue, mixed in with unrelated stories. The test is
whether it notices that this story holds the idea it needs. So the clue must carry the same idea as the real
solution, in a completely different everyday setting.

You get:
- STATEMENT: the real research finding.
- PRINCIPLE: the idea behind the researchers' solution.
- TRAP: the obvious wrong fix, which the problem rules out.
- PROBLEM: what the model is stuck on.
- ANSWER: what a correct reply to the problem says.

Write, in this order:

parts
- The PRINCIPLE split into its 2-4 parts, each short. Say who or what does each thing: for example, whether
  something happens by itself or someone makes it happen. That difference matters.
- Keep the kind of change exact, as the ANSWER has it: going back to how it was is not the same as dying,
  breaking or being thrown away; being held in is not the same as being kept out.
- If the principle lets everything in first, that is a part of its own: nothing is checked, sorted or turned
  away at the entrance.

mapping
- For each part: how it happens in the ANSWER, and what happens in your clue that plays the same role, done by
  the same kind of actor. A list of {"part": "...", "problem": "...", "clue": "..."}.

clue
- 2-3 short sentences: one small scene from everyday life (home, cooking, sport, farming, shops, travel,
  nature, ...), far from the problem's field and setting.
- Every part in `mapping` happens in it as an event, in the same role, done by the same kind of actor, with the
  same kind of change.
- If a part happens by itself, nothing and nobody in the scene makes it happen: no checker, detector, sorter,
  guard or helper. If everything is let in first, nothing in the scene checks things at the door or turns them
  away on arrival.
- Before replying, say to yourself in one sentence what a stranger who reads only the scene would think its
  trick is. If that is the TRAP, or anything other than the PRINCIPLE, pick a different scene.
- It is a scene, not a puzzle or a lesson: no question, no "You can't ...", no moral at the end, and none of
  the principle's own words. Show the idea; never name it.
- Share no key words with the PROBLEM, and do not follow its sentence order.

STATEMENT: Lithium-metal batteries short-circuit because needle-like metal deposits grow from the anode through the separator. Adding a salt that makes the deposits spread sideways instead of upward kept cells working for 1,000 cycles. (Science, 3 Aug 2026)
PRINCIPLE: Redirect the harmful growth instead of blocking it.
PROBLEM: Each time a new kind of rechargeable battery charges, sharp metal spikes grow from one side towards the other, and when one reaches across, the battery shorts out. A tougher wall between the two sides only delays the failure, and you can't stop the metal from building up while it charges. How can you keep the battery from shorting?
ANSWER: Change how the metal builds up so it spreads flat along its own side instead of growing towards the other side; then it can keep building up without ever reaching across.
