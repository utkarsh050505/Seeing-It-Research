You write test problems for a research benchmark. A language model will get your PROBLEM and try to solve it.
We want to see whether it reaches the idea behind a real research solution on its own.

You get:
- STATEMENT: a real research finding: what the researchers were stuck on, and how they got past it.
- PRINCIPLE: the deep idea behind their solution.
- TRAP: the obvious textbook answer, which is not what they did.

Write five things, in this order.

key_step
- One sentence: the thing a solver has to realise to reach the researchers' solution.
- Usually it is the heart of the fix. Sometimes it is spotting a hidden cause (for example, that the damage
  comes from the thing itself); then the problem describes only what is seen and asks what is going on and
  what would stop it.

hide
- The facts from the statement that would give the key step away, as a list. The problem must not contain
  any of them, not even reworded. If the key step is a hidden cause, the cause itself is on this list.

problem
- The situation from the statement, told as a puzzle someone is stuck on, ending with a question.
- 2-4 short sentences in plain words. Name things by what they are or do in everyday words ("a new kind of
  battery", not "a lithium-metal anode"), so a solver who misses the answer missed the idea, not the jargon.
- Say what the goal is and what stands in the way.
- The question must fit what the problem says. Do not describe the goal as already reached and then ask how to
  reach it. Ask one clear thing: how to reach the goal, or (for a hidden cause) what is going on and what
  would stop it.
- Rule out the TRAP and any other obvious answer: say it is not allowed ("You can't ...") or that it was tried
  and did not help.
- Every limit must still allow the researchers' own solution. Check it against each limit.
- It must not state, name or hint at the key step or the principle, and must contain nothing from `hide`.
- If the PRINCIPLE says where the harm or the effect comes from, finding that out IS the key step: the
  problem must not say or hint where it comes from (not even "it seems to produce something harmful"). Give
  only what is seen and ask what is going on and what would stop it.
- Use only facts from the statement, or what plainly follows from them. Invent no new facts or numbers.
- Leave hidden facts out. Never replace one with a different or untrue fact, and do not describe outcomes the
  statement does not give. When in doubt, ask how to reach the goal instead of describing the result.
- No field words a non-expert would not know: if only a specialist would say it, use everyday words instead.
- Do not mention the researchers, the study, the journal or the date.
- Before replying, read the problem as a stranger would. If the key step is the obvious next thought, it
  gives too much away: rewrite it.

answer
- 1-2 sentences in the problem's own words: what a correct reply says. It must rest on the principle.
- A reply that reaches this idea counts as solved, however it is worded.

reason
- 1-2 sentences: why this answer solves the problem, and how it follows the principle.
